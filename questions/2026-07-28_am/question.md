# The Topological Group That Must Be Discrete

Let $G$ be a topological group (a group that is also a topological space, where multiplication and inversion are continuous). Suppose $G$ has a subset $U$ that is open, and that $U$ contains no element other than the identity $e$ that has finite order (i.e., no non-identity element $g \in U$ satisfies $g^n = e$ for some $n \geq 1$).

Now consider a **different** scenario: suppose $G$ is a topological group and $\{e\}$ is an **open** set (the trivial subgroup is open).

**Prove that $G$ must be discrete.**

Then use this to answer: why is $\mathbb{Q}$ (with the subspace topology from $\mathbb{R}$) **not** a discrete topological group under addition, even though $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$ is a perfectly good group?
